If I could only own one knife for the rest of my life, it would be a gyuto. After more than a decade of cooking through everything from cheap stamped Western chef knives to hand-forged honyaki blades that cost more than my fridge, the gyuto is the one shape I keep coming back to. It does almost everything well, it feels right in the hand within minutes, and a good one will outlast you.

This guide is the result of years of buying, using, regretting, and re-buying. I've grouped picks by budget — $50, $100, $200, $300, $400 — because that's how most people actually shop. I'll tell you what each tier gets you, where the diminishing returns kick in, and which knives I would personally hand to a friend at each price point. No affiliate spin, no "best of everything." Just the ones I'd buy again.

## What is a gyuto, exactly?

"Gyuto" (牛刀) literally means "cow sword." It was Japan's answer to the French chef knife when Western cuisine started filtering into Japanese kitchens in the late Meiji era. The shape is unmistakably Western — curved belly, pointed tip, double-bevel edge — but everything else is Japanese: harder steel, thinner geometry, lighter weight, a sharper edge angle (typically 15 degrees per side instead of the Western 20).

### Gyuto vs. Western chef knife

A German chef knife like a Wüsthof Classic is a hammer. The blade is thick (around 2.5–3 mm at the spine), the steel is soft (HRC 56–58), and it's built to be ground on a steel and abused on a plastic board for thirty years. It works.

A gyuto is a scalpel. The spine is often 1.5–2 mm, the steel runs HRC 60–63, the edge geometry is acute, and it slices through a tomato by gravity alone. The trade-off: you cannot baton through chicken bones, you cannot scoop with the spine onto a metal pan, and you have to actually take care of it.

If you've never cooked with one, the first cut through an onion is genuinely startling.

### Length: 180, 210, 240 — which one?

This is the question I get asked most.

- **180 mm (7 in)**: Compact. Good for small hands, small boards, small kitchens. I find it short for anything bigger than a single chicken breast. If you're choosing between this and a santoku, get the santoku.
- **210 mm (8.25 in)**: The default. 90% of home cooks should buy a 210. It handles a butternut squash, breaks down a chicken, and still works on a 40 cm board. Every knife in this guide is available in 210.
- **240 mm (9.5 in)**: My personal pick. More knuckle clearance, faster through long vegetables, better leverage on hard squash. Needs at least a 45 cm cutting board. If you have the counter space and you cook a lot, jump to 240.
- **270 mm (10.6 in) and up**: Pro line cook territory. Don't.

If in doubt, 210. You can always buy a 240 later as a second knife.

## Steel: the four families you need to know

Japanese knife steel is a rabbit hole, and I've written a full [steel guide](/magazine/japanese-knife-steel-guide) if you want the deep dive. For this article, here's the cheat sheet.

### VG-10

A stainless steel from Takefu Special Steel. HRC 60–61. Holds an edge well, sharpens easily, doesn't rust. The "safe default" of Japanese knives. Tojiro DP, Sakai Takayuki standard line, Shun Classic — all VG-10 or close cousins. Excellent first knife steel.

### SG2 / R2 (powder metallurgy)

A powdered stainless steel made by Takefu (SG2) or Kobelco (R2 — same composition, different mill). HRC 62–64. Holds an edge much longer than VG-10, takes a sharper apex, more brittle if you abuse it. Found in Takamura, Ryusen, mid-range Tsunehisa, Hattori. Worth the upgrade if you sharpen yourself.

### Aogami (Blue carbon)

Hitachi Aogami #1 and #2. Not stainless. Will rust if you leave it wet. Takes the most ridiculous edge you've ever felt and holds it forever. Patinas dark grey-blue with use. The favorite of serious home cooks and traditionalists. Found in Tanaka, Yoshikane, Konosuke, Sakai Takayuki Aogami Super.

### Shirogami (White carbon)

Hitachi Shirogami #1 and #2. The purest carbon steel made. Even more reactive than Aogami, even sharper at peak, slightly less edge retention. The steel of Sakai sushi knives and Masamoto KS. If you sharpen, this is the dream.

If you're new and don't want to think about it: get VG-10. If you want to learn to sharpen: get Aogami. If you want maximum performance and don't mind a bit more maintenance: get SG2.

## The picks, by budget

### $50 tier: Tojiro DP Gyuto 210mm

The Tojiro DP has been the universal "first Japanese knife" recommendation for fifteen years and there's a reason it hasn't been dethroned. Three-layer construction with a VG-10 core sandwiched between softer stainless cladding, a black pakkawood Western handle, and a ground edge that's already shaving sharp out of the box. You can read my full [Tojiro DP review](/magazine/tojiro-dp-review) if you want the long version.

What you give up at this price: the handle is functional but plain, the fit-and-finish on the spine and choil is rough (round it off with sandpaper in five minutes), and the blade profile has a slightly more pronounced belly than purists like. None of these matter. It cuts.

**Buy this if**: this is your first Japanese knife, or you want a beater you don't have to baby.

### $100 tier: Mac Professional MTH-80

I almost wrote "still the Tojiro DP" here, because honestly the jump from $50 to $100 is the smallest in this entire guide. But if you're spending $100, the Mac Professional MTH-80 is the gyuto I'd hand you.

Mac is a Japanese company that has been making knives for the American market since the 1960s, and the Professional series is their workhorse. Proprietary high-carbon stainless at HRC 59–61, a thin grind that punches above the steel spec, and the most genuinely comfortable Western handle in the price range. The dimples on the blade are mostly cosmetic, but the geometry underneath them is excellent.

The downside: the steel is softer than VG-10, so the edge won't last quite as long. You'll touch it up on a ceramic rod weekly instead of monthly. In exchange you get an easier sharpener and a knife that takes a screaming edge.

**Buy this if**: you want a step up in handle comfort and blade balance from the Tojiro, without entering powder steel territory.

### $200 tier: Sakai Takayuki 33-Layer Damascus VG-10 Gyuto

This is where things start getting fun. At $200 you're buying a knife from a small Sakai workshop, hand-finished, with an actual aesthetic point of view.

The Sakai Takayuki 33-layer Damascus uses a VG-10 core (so the cutting performance is similar to the Tojiro) but the cladding is a mokume-style 33-layer stainless damascus that's both gorgeous and functional — the patterning helps food release. The handle is a proper octagonal Japanese-style ho-wood with a pakka ferrule, which sits in the hand differently from a riveted Western handle. Lighter, more forward-balanced, more agile.

The catch: the Japanese octagonal handle is a stylistic choice. If you've only ever held Western handles, it'll feel weird for a week. After that week, you may never go back.

**Buy this if**: you want your first "real" Japanese knife with traditional handle and visible smithing work.

### $300 tier: Misono UX10 Gyuto 240mm

The Misono UX10 is the knife that lives in pretty much every serious Japanese restaurant kitchen in the West. Swedish stainless steel (the same stuff used in surgical instruments) at HRC 59–60, a Western riveted handle with a perfectly weighted bolster, and a blade that's thinner and lighter than anything else with a Western handle in this price range.

I've used a UX10 240mm as my daily driver for stretches of two-plus years. It holds an edge longer than the Mac, sharpens faster than the Tojiro, and the balance point sits right under your pinch grip. It's boring in the best possible way — it just works, every day, for decades.

What you don't get for $300: a handmade aesthetic. The UX10 is machine-finished to a high standard. It looks like a tool. If you want jewelry, look elsewhere.

**Buy this if**: you want the most reliable workhorse in the guide and you don't care about traditional aesthetics.

### $400 tier: Masamoto KS Gyuto 240mm

The Masamoto KS is one of the most quietly legendary knives in the world. White #2 carbon steel core, soft iron cladding, water-quenched in a Tokyo workshop that has been doing this since the late 1800s. The KS line was famously the favorite of generations of New York fine-dining chefs.

What you're paying for: the geometry. The blade has a perfectly flat heel for push-cutting, a gentle curve toward the tip, and a grind so thin behind the edge that the knife wedges less than knives twice the price. The white steel takes an apex finer than any stainless, and you'll feel it the first time you cut a ripe tomato — the skin parts under the weight of the blade alone.

The downsides are real. White #2 will rust if you leave it wet for ten minutes. The carbon cladding will patina to a mottled blue-grey. You will need a sharpening stone — a ceramic rod won't cut it. If you're not willing to dry the knife immediately after each use, do not buy this.

**Buy this if**: you sharpen on stones, you cook every day, and you want a knife you can hand to your kids.

## A note on honorable mentions

The Takamura R2 deserves its own paragraph. If you specifically want maximum edge retention with a Western handle and you don't care about brand prestige, the Takamura R2 Migaki is arguably the best-cutting knife in this entire guide for under $250. The reason it's not my main $200 pick is availability — Takamura runs in small batches and stock is unpredictable. If you can find one, buy it.

## How to actually choose

If you're paralyzed:

1. **Brand new to Japanese knives, never sharpened a knife in your life**: Tojiro DP 210mm. $55. Done. Read my [first knife guide](/magazine/how-to-choose-first-japanese-knife) for sharpening basics.
2. **Some Japanese knife experience, ready to upgrade**: Sakai Takayuki 33-layer or Misono UX10 depending on whether you want traditional or Western feel.
3. **You sharpen on stones already**: Masamoto KS. Skip the middle tiers.
4. **You cook professionally**: Misono UX10 240mm for the line, Masamoto KS for prep.

Whatever you buy, get a [petty](/magazine/best-petty-knives-2026) within six months. A gyuto plus a 150mm petty handles 95% of home cooking.

## FAQ

**Do I need to baby a Japanese gyuto?**
The stainless ones (Tojiro, Mac, Sakai Takayuki VG-10, Misono UX10) — no. Wash by hand, dry it, store it on a magnetic strip or in a saya. That's it. Carbon ones (Masamoto KS, Aogami knives) — yes. Wipe between cuts of acidic foods, dry immediately, oil if storing for more than a week.

**Can I put it in the dishwasher?**
No. Not even the stainless ones. Detergent micro-pits the steel and the rack will chip the edge.

**Why is the handle on Japanese knives so different?**
Two traditions. The octagonal/D-shape ho-wood handle is traditional Japanese — light, forward-balanced, replaceable. The Western riveted handle was adopted for the gyuto specifically because it was Japan's "Western" knife. Both work. It's preference.

**Is more expensive always better?**
No, and the curve flattens hard above $300. The Tojiro DP cuts onions about 85% as well as the Masamoto KS. You're paying for steel, geometry refinement, and craftsmanship at the high end — real things, but not linearly.

**Can I sharpen these on a pull-through sharpener?**
Don't. Pull-through sharpeners use carbide or ceramic wheels at fixed angles (usually 20°) which don't match the 15° geometry of a Japanese knife and will tear the thin edge. Get a 1000/6000 combination whetstone. It's $40 and it'll last decades.

**What about left-handed cooks?**
Every gyuto in this guide is double-bevel and works equally well in either hand. Single-bevel knives (yanagiba, deba, usuba) need handed versions, but gyutos do not.

**Are these all made in Japan?**
Yes. The Tojiro is made in Tsubame-Sanjo (Niigata prefecture). Sakai Takayuki and Masamoto are made in Sakai (Osaka prefecture, the capital of Japanese kitchen knife forging). Mac and Misono are made in Seki (Gifu prefecture). All three regions have centuries of swordsmithing heritage.

**Do I need a specific cutting board?**
End-grain wood (maple, cherry, walnut) is ideal — it preserves the edge longest. Edge-grain wood is fine. Soft plastic (polypropylene) is acceptable. Hard plastic (HDPE) is okay but harder on the edge. Glass, marble, ceramic, granite — never. They will destroy the edge in a single use.

## Final word

Buy one knife, learn it, sharpen it, use it for a year before you buy the next one. Most home cooks are better served by a $55 Tojiro they've used 500 times than a $400 Masamoto sitting on a magnetic strip looking pretty.

The best gyuto for you is the one you'll actually pick up tonight.
