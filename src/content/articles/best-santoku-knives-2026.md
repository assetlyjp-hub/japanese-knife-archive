If you walk into almost any Japanese home kitchen, the knife sitting on the magnetic strip is probably a santoku. It is the workhorse of the country it comes from, and over the last decade it has quietly become the default "nice knife" people buy when they want to upgrade from a cheap stamped chef's knife. I have spent years cooking with santoku of every price point, and in this guide I want to walk you through what the blade actually does well, where it falls short, and which ones are worth your money in 2026.

## What "santoku" actually means

The word *santoku* (三徳) translates roughly to "three virtues." You will see a lot of disagreement online about what those three virtues are. Some say it refers to mastery of three tasks: slicing, dicing, and mincing. Others say it points to the three main ingredients a home cook handles — meat, fish, and vegetables. A third interpretation ties it to three cutting techniques. Honestly, all of these are reasonable, and the truth is the name was a marketing-friendly way of saying "this one knife handles everything a home cook needs."

That is the key idea. The santoku was designed in mid-20th-century Japan as a Western-influenced general-purpose knife for the home, replacing the traditional nakiri (a straight vegetable cleaver) and the smaller utility knives most households used. It is a do-everything blade aimed squarely at people who want *one* knife to reach for, not a drawer full of specialists.

## The shape: flat profile and a sheepsfoot tip

Two features define the santoku silhouette, and both matter for how it cuts.

**A flat (or nearly flat) cutting edge.** Unlike a Western chef's knife, which has a pronounced curved belly built for rocking, the santoku's edge is mostly straight. The blade meets the cutting board along a long, flat line. This rewards a different motion entirely — more on that below.

**A sheepsfoot tip.** Instead of coming to a sharp upswept point like a gyuto or a chef's knife, the spine of a santoku curves sharply down at the front to meet the edge. The result looks a little like a sheep's hoof, hence the name. This gives you a blunt, controllable nose rather than a needle point.

The blade is also usually shorter and a touch taller than a Western chef's knife. That extra height gives you knuckle clearance over the board and a wide flat face that is genuinely useful for scooping chopped vegetables and carrying them to the pan.

## How you actually cut with one: the push cut

Here is the single most important thing to understand before you buy a santoku. **It is not a rocking knife.** If you try to rock-chop with it the way you would with a French chef's knife, you will feel the flat edge slap the board and you will be frustrated within five minutes.

The santoku is built for a **push cut** (and its cousin, the tap-chop). You lift the whole blade, move it slightly forward and down through the food, and let the flat edge make full contact with the board in one clean stroke. Think "guillotine with a small forward push" rather than "rock back and forth." Because the edge is flat, every part of it touches the board at once, so you get complete, decisive cuts of vegetables, herbs, and boneless proteins.

Once it clicks, it is fast and satisfying. The flat profile is actually *better* than a curved blade for getting through a pile of onions or carrots cleanly, because you are not leaving little uncut hinges the way a rocking curve sometimes does. But it is a different muscle memory, and that adjustment is the number one reason people either love or bounce off the santoku. ## Santoku vs gyuto: which should a home cook buy?

This is the question I get more than any other, so let me be direct. The gyuto is the Japanese take on the Western chef's knife: longer (typically 210mm), with a curved belly that lets you rock, and a pointed tip for detail work. The santoku is shorter (165–180mm), flatter, and blunter at the tip.

Here is how I think about it:

- **Buy a santoku if** you have a smaller kitchen, smaller hands, a smaller cutting board, or you simply prefer an up-and-down chopping motion. It is also the friendlier knife for people who are nervous around a long pointed blade, and it stores well in tight spaces.
- **Buy a gyuto if** you cook a lot of protein, want a longer blade for slicing roasts or breaking down larger items, like to rock-chop, or want one knife that scales up to serious volume.

Neither is "better." They are answers to slightly different questions. I have written a full head-to-head if you want the deep version — see [gyuto vs santoku](/magazine/gyuto-vs-santoku) — but the short answer is that for a lot of home cooks who mostly chop vegetables and the occasional chicken breast, the santoku is the more comfortable everyday tool. ## Length: 165mm or 180mm?

Santoku come in a fairly narrow size range, and the two you will encounter most are 165mm and 180mm.

- **165mm (~6.5")** is the traditional, "true" santoku size. It is nimble, easy to control, and ideal for smaller hands and smaller boards. If you are stepping up from a paring knife or you mostly do detailed prep, this is a comfortable choice.
- **180mm (~7")** gives you a little more reach and is closer to a small chef's knife. If you cook for a family, handle larger vegetables like cabbage and squash regularly, or just have bigger hands, the extra length is welcome.

There is no wrong answer here. I personally keep a 180mm on the strip because I like the reach, but I happily recommend 165mm to anyone with a compact kitchen. When in doubt, go 165mm — it is the size the knife was designed around, and you can always add a longer blade later.

## The granton (dimpled) edge: helpful or marketing?

You have almost certainly seen santoku with a row of oval dimples ground into the side of the blade. This is called a **granton edge** (or *kullenschliff*), and it is marketed as a non-stick feature: the dimples are supposed to create little air pockets that stop food from sticking to the blade.

Here is my honest take after years of using both kinds. **It helps a little, with some foods, sometimes.** When you are slicing something dense and starchy like a potato or a sweet potato, dimples genuinely reduce suction and food releases a bit more easily. For most other tasks — onions, herbs, meat — you will barely notice a difference. It is not a deal-breaker either way.

So do not pay a meaningful premium *just* for dimples, and do not avoid a knife you otherwise love because it lacks them. Treat the granton edge as a minor convenience, not a core feature. One small downside: dimples are slightly more fiddly to wipe clean, and on cheaper knives they are sometimes stamped rather than properly ground, which does nothing useful at all.

## My tier picks for 2026

I have grouped these by budget. Every one of these is a genuinely good knife at its price — the differences are about steel quality, fit and finish, and how long the edge holds.

### ~$50 — Tojiro DP Santoku 170mm

The Tojiro DP line is the knife I recommend more than any other to people buying their first real Japanese blade. It uses a VG10 stainless core clad in softer stainless steel, takes a keen edge, holds it respectably, and shrugs off the abuse of a busy home kitchen. The handle is a simple Western-style bolstered design — not beautiful, but durable and dishwasher-surviving (though you should never put any good knife in a dishwasher). At this price there is nothing else I would rather hand a beginner. It punches far above its cost.

### ~$100 — Global G-46 Santoku

Love it or hate it, the all-stainless Global G-46 is an icon. The seamless one-piece construction means there are no handle seams to trap bacteria, the dimpled blade is light and fast, and the distinctive dimpled handle is more comfortable than it looks once your hand learns it. The steel (Cromova 18) is softer than VG10, so it dulls a bit faster, but it is forgiving and easy to bring back. If you want a hygienic, modern, no-wood-to-baby knife, this is the pick. The polarizing handle is the main thing to try before you commit.

### ~$150 — Shun Premier Santoku 180mm

This is where you start paying for beauty as well as performance. The Shun Premier uses VG-MAX steel with a hand-hammered (*tsuchime*) finish that genuinely helps food release, wrapped in a gorgeous walnut-colored PakkaWood handle. It is sharp out of the box, holds an edge well, and feels special every time you pick it up. Shun's customer service and free sharpening program are a real bonus. The trade-off is that the harder steel is a little less tolerant of abuse, and the pointed-ish tip and finish demand hand-washing and care.

### ~$250 — Artisan single-bevel-adjacent / premium double-bevel

At this tier you are buying from smaller Japanese makers — think Sakai- or Echizen-forged blades in steels like Aogami (Blue) or SG2/R2 powder steel, often with a beautiful Damascus cladding and a traditional octagonal *wa* handle. These knives take a screamingly sharp edge and hold it for a long time, and they are a joy to use and to own. But this is firmly enthusiast territory: many of these steels are carbon or carbon-cored, which means they can react and need drying and a little oil. Do not start here. Earn your way up once you know you love the format and you are comfortable maintaining a blade. ## The honest downsides of a santoku

I would be doing you a disservice if I only sold you on it. Here is where the santoku genuinely loses to other knives.

- **The flat profile fights rock-choppers.** If you have spent twenty years rocking a chef's knife, the adjustment is real and some people never warm to it.
- **The blunt tip is a liability for detail work.** Coring a tomato, scoring fat, or doing fine point work is harder than with a gyuto's needle tip. I keep a small petty knife around precisely for this — see my [petty knife guide](/magazine/best-petty-knives-2026).
- **The shorter length caps you.** Slicing a roast, a large watermelon, or carving a chicken is awkward on a 165–180mm blade. It is not built for big work.
- **It is not a cleaver.** Do not chop through bones or frozen food. Hard Japanese steel is thin and brittle compared to a German chef's knife, and you will chip the edge.

None of these are dealbreakers for a home cook who mostly preps vegetables and boneless proteins. They are just the trade-offs you accept in exchange for a light, sharp, comfortable everyday blade.

## FAQ

**Is a santoku good for beginners?**
Yes, with one caveat: you have to learn the push-cut motion. Once you do, it is one of the most beginner-friendly Japanese knives, and the Tojiro DP at ~$50 is an ideal starting point. Pair it with my [buying guide](/magazine/japanese-knife-buying-guide) for the full picture.

**Santoku or chef's knife — do I need both?**
No. Pick one as your primary blade. Many home cooks are perfectly served by a santoku alone, plus a small petty knife for detail tasks. Add a gyuto later only if you find yourself wanting more length.

**What length santoku should I buy?**
Start at 165mm if you have a small kitchen or smaller hands; go 180mm if you cook for a family or want a bit more reach. Both are correct choices.

**Do the dimples (granton edge) really stop food sticking?**
A little, mainly with dense starchy vegetables like potatoes. Do not pay a premium for dimples alone, and do not avoid a knife that lacks them.

**Can I rock-chop with a santoku?**
Not really — the flat edge resists it. The santoku wants a push cut or tap chop. If you love rocking, buy a gyuto instead.

**Is a santoku Japanese or Western?**
Both, in a sense. It is a Japanese knife born from Western influence — a 20th-century general-purpose blade designed to replace the traditional nakiri and utility knives in Japanese homes.

**Can I cut meat with a santoku?**
Yes, boneless meat and poultry are fine. Never cut through bone or frozen food — the thin, hard edge will chip.

**Stainless or carbon steel for a santoku?**
For an everyday home santoku, stainless (VG10, VG-MAX) is the low-maintenance choice and what I recommend for most people. Carbon takes a finer edge but reacts and needs drying. My [steel guide](/magazine/japanese-knife-steel-guide) covers the trade-offs in depth.

## The bottom line

The santoku earns its place as Japan's default home knife for a good reason: it is light, sharp, comfortable, and it handles the vast majority of everyday prep with a clean, decisive motion. If you cook mostly vegetables and boneless proteins, value a compact knife, and are willing to learn a push cut, it may be the only knife you ever really need. Start with the Tojiro DP, master the motion, and only climb the price ladder once you know you love the format.
