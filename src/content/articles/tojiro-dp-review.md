I bought my first Tojiro DP gyuto in early 2021. I still have it. It still cuts well. I've used it through five Christmases, three Thanksgivings, hundreds of weeknight dinners, two moves, and one accidental dishwasher cycle (don't). It's been sharpened maybe twenty times, dropped twice on a tile floor, and at one point I used it to pry the lid off a paint can — which I will not confess to in print.

This is the knife that taught me what a Japanese kitchen knife actually feels like. It's also the knife I recommend to literally every friend who asks me where to start. Five years in, here's what I think holds up, what doesn't, and whether the universal "buy a Tojiro DP" advice still applies in 2026.

## Who makes Tojiro?

Tojiro is a brand of Fujitora Industry, a knifemaker headquartered in Tsubame-Sanjo, Niigata prefecture. If you don't know Tsubame-Sanjo, you should — it's the metalworking capital of Japan. The region has been making nails, files, and cutlery since the Edo period, and today produces the majority of Japan's stainless flatware and a huge chunk of its kitchen knives. Snow Peak (the camping gear brand) is from the same town. So is Yoshida Metal Industry (Globalsknives are theirs).

Fujitora was founded in 1953 and started the Tojiro brand specifically to bring Japanese-quality knives to the export market at an accessible price. The DP series (DP stands for "double-action processing," referring to the cladding method) launched in the late 1990s and has been their flagship workhorse line ever since. In a world of marketing-driven knife brands, Tojiro is refreshingly engineering-driven — they publish actual steel hardness specs, they don't make absurd "1,200-year tradition" claims, and the prices have stayed roughly flat for two decades despite inflation.

## The DP construction in detail

The Tojiro DP is what's called a "san-mai" (three-layer) construction.

- **Core**: VG-10 stainless steel, hardened to HRC 60. This is the actual cutting edge.
- **Cladding**: 13Cr stainless (a softer, tougher chromium stainless) sandwiching the VG-10 core on both sides.

The reason for the sandwich is twofold. First, VG-10 is harder and more brittle than the cladding, so the soft outer layers absorb lateral stress and protect the core from chipping if you torque the blade. Second, the soft cladding sharpens away faster than the core, so over time the VG-10 edge "stands proud" and you're really only sharpening the thin band of hard steel right at the apex. This is what makes a clad knife both sharper and more durable than a monosteel one of the same hardness.

The handle is a riveted Western "yo" handle in dark brown pakkawood. It's not beautiful, but the rivets are flush, the bolster is rounded, and after a week your hand stops noticing it. There's no bolster on the heel side, which means you can sharpen all the way to the back of the blade — a real practical advantage over most Western chef knives.

## The DP lineup

The DP series isn't just a gyuto. There are something like fifteen knives in the lineup. The three you actually want to know about:

### Tojiro DP Gyuto 210mm

The default. This is the knife in this review unless I say otherwise. 8.25-inch chef knife profile, weighs around 195g, balance point right at the bolster. Handles 90% of home cooking tasks.

### Tojiro DP Santoku 170mm

The "Asian profile" alternative — flatter belly, sheepsfoot tip, no point. Better for the rocking-chop-resistant person who push-cuts everything. If you've never used a Japanese knife before and the thought of a long pointed blade makes you nervous, get the santoku instead. I have one too. It does most of what the gyuto does, just with less reach. See [gyuto vs. santoku](/articles/gyuto-vs-santoku) for the full comparison.

### Tojiro DP Petty 150mm

The little brother every gyuto needs. 6-inch utility knife for the small jobs the gyuto is too big for — peeling shallots, deveining shrimp, halving cherry tomatoes, butchering a single chicken thigh. If you buy the gyuto, get the petty within six months. They're $35 and they fill the gap perfectly. (There's also a 120mm DP petty for fruit-knife-scale tasks.)

There are also Tojiro DP slicers, nakiri, bread knives, and bonings. The non-gyuto/santoku/petty knives in the line are fine but not the deal that the main three are.

## Five years of ownership

### Out of the box

The factory edge on a Tojiro DP is genuinely sharp. Not "wow this is sharper than my Wüsthof" sharp — actually sharp. It will shave arm hair, push-cut printer paper, and slice a tomato by gravity alone. This is rare in any knife under $100.

The fit and finish has rough spots. The spine and choil (the corner where the heel meets the handle) come from the factory with sharp 90-degree edges that will dig into your pinch grip after twenty minutes. Five minutes of work with 400-grit then 800-grit sandpaper will round these off and the knife will feel dramatically better. This is a known issue and a known fix. Tojiro could solve it for $2 of factory labor and they don't, which is annoying.

The handle had one minor sharp seam where the pakkawood meets the bolster. A few passes with 1000-grit sandpaper and it was gone. Total finishing work: maybe ten minutes.

### Year 1

Used it daily. Sharpened it three times that year — once at six months, again at nine, again at twelve. Sharpening was easy: 1000 grit, 6000 grit, leather strop. The VG-10 takes about 8 minutes per side from dull to scary sharp. Edge held a working sharpness (still slices tomato skin) for about four to six weeks of daily use between touch-ups on a ceramic rod.

### Year 2-3

This is when I started cooking more seriously. Took on prepping for dinner parties, batch cooking, etc. The DP gyuto handled it. I was impressed how it didn't develop chips even when I knocked the edge against an enameled cast iron pan a few times.

The handle started showing wear — small dents from being dropped in the sink, a slight darkening of the pakkawood from oils. Cosmetic only. Tight as ever.

### Year 4

I bought a Misono UX10 240mm and started using that as my daily driver for prep. The Tojiro got demoted to the "spouse uses it" knife and the camping/Airbnb knife. It still gets used three times a week.

### Year 5 (now)

Still going. The blade has lost maybe 0.5mm of width from sharpening. The handle has a couple of dings. The edge sharpens up the same as it did on day one. There is no reason this knife shouldn't last another twenty years.

## Edge retention, in real terms

This is the question everyone asks and most reviews lie about.

A fresh sharpening on the Tojiro DP gives me about three to four weeks of cooking before I need a touch-up on a ceramic rod, and about six months before I need to go back to stones. "Cooking" here means I cook five or six dinners a week from scratch — call it 8 hours of cutting time per month.

For comparison, the Masamoto KS in the same usage holds a working edge about 2x longer, and the Takamura R2 about 2.5x longer. The Tojiro is the floor of "good Japanese steel performance" but it's still a country mile better than any Western knife at the same price.

What kills the Tojiro edge fastest, in order: cutting on glass or ceramic boards (do not), cutting frozen food (use a different knife), and lateral torque while cutting hard squash (let the blade do the work). On a wood board with normal food, it's basically immortal.

## Sharpening

VG-10 sharpens beautifully on Japanese waterstones. I use a 1000/6000 combination stone (cost: $40, the Suehiro Cerax is fine, the Shapton Glass is better) and a leather strop loaded with chromium oxide.

The process: about 15 strokes per side on the 1000 grit until I raise a burr along the entire edge, then 30-40 strokes per side on the 6000 grit, then 20 strokes per side on the strop. Total time: 15 minutes from setup to "shaving sharp." After a year of practice you can do it in 10.

If you've never sharpened a knife, this is intimidating. It is also one of the most satisfying skills in cooking. There are excellent free YouTube tutorials (search "Korin Naoto sharpening" — the gold standard). The first time you do it on a Tojiro and the knife comes out sharper than the day you bought it, you understand why people get into this hobby.

## Western handle ergonomics — the surprise

I'll be honest, when I started buying Japanese knives I assumed the traditional octagonal ho-wood handle was the obviously superior choice. After all, it's lighter, forward-balanced, more "authentic." Right?

Five years in, I'm less sure. The Tojiro's Western handle has one massive practical advantage: it's idiot-proof. There is exactly one way to grip it, the rivets give you a tactile reference for orientation in low light, and it doesn't loosen when wet. The traditional Japanese handle is gorgeous and lighter, but I've had two octagonal handles loosen on me over the years (one needed re-burning of the tang).

For a daily driver in a busy kitchen with kids, the Western handle on the Tojiro is, weirdly, the right call. I didn't expect to feel that way.

## What it does NOT do

Let's be honest about the limits.

- **It's not a butcher's knife.** Don't go through chicken bones. Don't try to split a duck. Use a [cleaver or bone-cleaver for that](/articles/japanese-knife-buying-guide).
- **It's not maximum-edge-retention steel.** If you sharpen yourself, fine. If you want to send it out twice a year and forget about it, the steel will be acceptable, not exceptional. Consider Takamura R2 if edge retention is your priority.
- **It's not pretty.** No damascus pattern, no engravings, plain black handle. It looks like a tool, because it is.
- **The fit-and-finish needs the sandpaper fix.** Out of the box the spine and choil are unfinished. This is a known annoyance.
- **It will not impress chef friends.** It impresses cooks. There is a difference.

## Tojiro DP vs. the competition

### Tojiro DP vs. Mac Professional MTH-80

The Mac is twice the price ($110 vs $55) and the immediate question is whether it's twice the knife. Honestly, no. The Mac has a more comfortable handle out of the box, slightly thinner geometry, and a better polish. But the steel doesn't hold an edge as long as the Tojiro's VG-10, and the actual cutting performance is closer than the price gap suggests. If you're tight on money, the Tojiro. If you want something you don't have to sand-paper, the Mac.

### Tojiro DP vs. Shun Classic

The Shun Classic is also VG-10, three-layer construction, and around $160 — three times the price of the Tojiro. The cladding is a 33-layer damascus, the handle is a beautiful D-shape pakkawood, and the fit and finish is genuinely excellent. Performance-wise, on a cutting board, the Tojiro and Shun do the same things. You're paying $100 for the aesthetics and the brand. If you value either, fine. If you don't, save the money. (See [Shun vs Global vs Miyabi](/articles/shun-vs-global-vs-miyabi) for the full comparison of premium Western-marketed Japanese knives.)

### Tojiro DP vs. Global G-2

The Global G-2 is the iconic "Japanese knife" most Americans recognize — the all-stainless one with the dimpled handle. It's a CROMOVA 18 monosteel at HRC 56–58, which is significantly softer than the Tojiro's VG-10. The Global has a better-balanced handle and the all-metal construction is dishwasher-safe (don't, but it survives). The Tojiro takes a sharper edge and holds it longer. If you cook a lot, Tojiro. If you don't cook much and you want something low-maintenance, Global.

## Should you buy one in 2026?

Yes. The Tojiro DP gyuto remains the highest-floor, lowest-ceiling knife in the entire Japanese knife market, and the gap to anything significantly better is bigger than the gap to anything worse.

If this is your first Japanese knife: buy the 210mm gyuto. Round off the spine and choil with sandpaper. Get a 1000/6000 whetstone, learn to sharpen, and use it daily for a year before you buy your next knife. You will end up cooking more, cooking better, and developing actual knife skills — which is what this whole hobby is supposed to be about anyway.

## FAQ

**Is the Tojiro DP a "real" Japanese knife?**
Yes. Made in Tsubame-Sanjo, designed and engineered by Fujitora, with VG-10 steel from Takefu Special Steel. It's not hand-forged by a single master in a tiny workshop, but it's a genuine Japanese knife from a serious Japanese maker.

**Will it rust?**
The cutting edge (VG-10) is stainless and won't rust under normal use. The cladding (13Cr) is also stainless. The exposed tang inside the handle could theoretically rust if water sits there for years — wash and dry as you would any knife.

**Can I leave it in a drawer?**
You can, but the edge will be damaged by hitting other utensils. Get a magnetic strip ($15) or a saya (knife sheath, $10) and the edge will last 3x longer between sharpenings.

**Why is the choil sharp out of the box?**
Cost-cutting. Tojiro grinds the heel area to a finish that's "acceptable" rather than "comfortable." Five minutes with sandpaper fixes it. This is the only legitimate complaint about this knife.

**How often should I sharpen it?**
Touch up on a ceramic rod every 2-3 weeks of daily use. Full sharpening on a whetstone every 4-6 months for a home cook, every 6-8 weeks for a serious daily user.

**Is there a single-bevel version?**
No. The DP line is all double-bevel Western/Asian-profile knives. If you want single-bevel traditional knives (yanagiba, deba, usuba) Tojiro makes them in their "Shirogami" line, but those are different products entirely.

**What's the difference between DP and DP3?**
"DP3" sometimes appears in product names — it's just an internal SKU revision, not a different knife. The actual product is the same.

**Can I gift one?**
Yes, but with a caveat — Japanese tradition says gifting a sharp blade "cuts" the relationship. The workaround is to ask the recipient for a small coin in exchange, which technically makes it a sale rather than a gift. Silly tradition, but if your recipient is into Japanese culture they'll appreciate the gesture.

## Final word

The Tojiro DP gyuto is the knife I have personally recommended to more friends than any other piece of kitchen equipment, full stop. Five years in, mine still cuts beautifully, sharpens easily, and shows no sign of giving up. At $55 it's not a budget knife — it's a serious knife that happens to be cheap.

Buy it. Learn it. Sharpen it. You'll know in a year whether you want to upgrade.
